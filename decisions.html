Open decisions

Every purple design note in the mock-up links to one of these. Each one sets out the question, the options, and a suggestion to react to.

The 5 to decide first

These shape the build. Most of the others can be settled during or after the pilot.

  1. B2 Platform: Webflow and Memberstack, or a GOV.UK Frontend site we host ourselves
  2. A1 Sign-in: family ID and password, or an emailed link, and who creates the IDs
  3. C1 Updating content: how PUBLIC edits guides with no developer
  4. D1 Engagement data: Google Analytics, or our own record of activity per family ID
  5. S3 Screenshot pipeline: our own form and storage, or Qualtrics

A. Access, and keeping the groups apart

A1. How do families sign in, and how is access really enforced?

The mock-up's sign-in is fake and runs in the browser. The real one must check every request on the server, so a forwarded link or a guessed web address shows nothing to someone who isn't signed in. (The tech spec says content should not be "viewable if a link is forwarded".)

  • Family ID and password (as in the spec and the mock-up): works without an email address and can go in a paper letter. But passwords get forgotten, and resetting one needs an email or a helpdesk.
  • Emailed sign-in link (no password): nothing to forget. Needs an email address for every family, and IFF holds those.
  • Both: a family ID in the letter, then a sign-in link sent to that family's email address.

Suggestion: family ID and password, printed in the onboarding pack by IFF at randomisation, with IFF as the reset route. Use one ID per family, not per child. [Check: can the ID be the same one IFF uses in the surveys, so the data joins up without extra matching?]

A2. Should the public page show a sign-in button?

Control families will see it too. The mock-up keeps it, with the line "If you have not been given sign-in details, you do not need to do anything". The alternative is a sign-in page that only has a direct link, sent to treatment families.

Suggestion: keep it, with the neutral wording. It's less confusing for treatment families who arrive through the public page.

A3. What exactly can control families and the public see?

The public page in the mock-up has: the study name, who is running it, the dates, a contact and the privacy notice. It says nothing about phones. Table 3 in the deliverables doc gives control families the "common core": welcome, partners, scale and timing, contact points, payments.

Suggestion: keep this page minimal, and leave contact points and payments to IFF's letters. Confirm with IFF and Cambridge that this is enough for informed consent.

A4. The public name of the study

If "SMART" stands for something with "smartphone" in it, the name alone tells control families what the study is about. The privacy notice and the web address (for example, smartstudy.org.uk) have the same problem.

Suggestion: use a neutral public name and web address. Keep "smartphone" for signed-in pages. The same applies to the logo and the public illustration, which show a sun, a family and a school, but no phones.

A5. Webinar recordings and other participants

Recordings may show other parents' names, faces or questions.

Suggestion: record only the presenter. Read questions out without names, or cut the Q&A from the upload.

B. Brand, platform and accessibility

B1. How "DfE" should it look?

The site will not be on GOV.UK, so it cannot use the GOV.UK crown, the GOV.UK header or the GDS Transport font. Families also respond better to something warm than to something that looks like a tax form.

The mock-up now has its own study identity: the SMART logo, the Nunito typeface (rounded and very readable), a warm palette anchored on DfE blue (#003a69), icons and illustrations. Underneath, it still uses GOV.UK Frontend for forms, error messages, accordions and tabs, so we keep their accessibility testing.

  • More playful: brighter colours and more illustration. Better for children's materials, but it can feel less official to parents.
  • Where it is now: friendly, with DfE blue and plenty of white space. It's credible without looking like government.
  • Closer to GOV.UK (the first version): the most official, but the least warm.

Suggestion: the current middle ground. Check it with parents in the pilot user testing.

B5. Logo, partner logos and illustrations

  • Study logo: the mark is a sun, a parent and a child. It deliberately shows no phone, so the logo doesn't hint at the intervention on the public page (see A4). We could keep it, refine it, or have a designer create one alongside the printed onboarding pack.
  • Partner logos (DfE, IFF, Cambridge, PUBLIC): the dashed boxes are placeholders. Each needs the organisation's permission and follows its brand guidelines. DfE's logo in particular goes through DfE's communications team. Having all four logos is reassuring to parents, but it's more to agree.
  • Illustrations were drawn for this mock-up, in one style and with people of different skin tones. For launch: keep and extend them, commission an illustrator (who could also make the children's video), or license a set. Stock photos of families are cheaper, but look generic and date quickly.

Suggestion: commission one illustrator for the site, the printed pack and the animated video, so everything families see looks like it belongs together. Ask partners for logos now, as approvals take time.

B2. Which platform?

  • Webflow with Memberstack (preferred in the deliverables doc): quick to build, visual editor, no developer needed for updates. However: the GOV.UK components would have to be rebuilt in Webflow (so their accessibility testing doesn't come with them), and both companies are US-based, so the DPA and data location need checking.
  • A GOV.UK Frontend site we host ourselves (what this mock-up is): for example, Eleventy with the x-govuk plugin, content in Markdown, edited through a web-based CMS (Decap or Pages CMS), hosted in the UK, with a small sign-in service. It looks and works like GOV.UK and we control the data. It needs a developer to set up and to fix anything that breaks.
  • WordPress with a membership plugin, on UK hosting (the alternative in the deliverables doc): familiar editing, but plugins need ongoing security updates.

Suggestion: decide after the DPIA says where login and screenshot data can be stored. If UK-only, go with the self-hosted GOV.UK Frontend site. If not, Webflow is the fastest route and the mock-up's layout can be rebuilt there.

B3. Accessibility testing

Test against WCAG 2.2 AA, with automated checks (such as axe) and manual testing with a screen reader and keyboard, before the pilot. Publish an accessibility statement.

B4. Languages

The spec says English plus the main community languages, to be confirmed from the sample. Translating the guides doubles the work every time a phone update changes a step.

Suggestion: English only for the pilot. For the main trial, translate the set-up guides and add translated subtitles to the videos. Keep the rest in English with a "need help in another language?" contact.

C. Content, and keeping it up to date

C1. How PUBLIC updates the guides without a developer

Whatever the platform, store each guide as structured content, not one long page:

Suggested content model
ItemFields
GuideTitle, who it's for (restrict or delay, phone type), time needed, "checked on" OS version and date, version number, video, list of steps, change log
StepTitle, instructions, picture (screenshot or drawn screen), video start time, "updated" note (optional)
Allowed appName, why it's allowed, date added. Shown in every guide.
UpdateDate, group, text, link. Shown in "What's new" and reused in the newsletter.
FAQQuestion, answer, group

In Webflow, each of these is a CMS Collection. In a self-hosted site, each is a Markdown file edited through a CMS. Either way, changing a step means editing a form field, not code, and the web page, PDF and "What's new" all update together.

Suggestion: agree this model before the build, and set up a named editor and a reviewer, so one person makes a change and a second person checks it on a real phone.

C2. Real screenshots or drawn screens?

Direction agreed: drawn screens. Every screen in the three guides is drawn from a short description in content/guidance.js, following the team's iOS screenshots (layout, labels, row order). The coral outline and label show what to tap, and a red dashed outline shows what not to tick.

  • They're vector, so they stay sharp on screen, on paper and in the PDF.
  • Changing a label after a software update means changing one word, then rebuilding.
  • The Android screens have no reference screenshots yet, so they're marked "Draft: check on a device".

Still to test: whether parents recognise the drawn screens as their own phone. Test this in the pilot user-testing sessions (16 to 20 November).

C12. How the guide gets edited: website, Word and Figma

One content file (content/guidance.js) builds all three versions with npm run build:guides:

  • Microsite: the three route pages.
  • Word: exports/SMART-restrictions-guidance.docx, for reviewing, signing off and printing. Its text is fully editable. The screens are sharp images.
  • Figma: exports/figma/route-a.svg (and b, c) are whole-route boards, and assets/guides/screens/ holds each screen on its own. Drag them into Figma: the text stays editable, in Inter.

Changes don't flow back automatically. Edits made in Word or Figma have to be copied into the content file by hand.

Suggestion: treat the content file as the master copy. Use Word for review and sign-off, and Figma for designing new screens. After sign-off, one person copies the changes in and rebuilds. If the build moves to Webflow, the same structure (route → step → screen) becomes CMS collections.

C3. How we find out a guide is out of date

There are three sources: a check of every guide on real devices within a week of each major iOS or Android release (iOS in September, with smaller updates throughout the year); "tell us which step" reports from parents; and helpdesk emails. Each fix gets a change log entry and, if it matters, a "What's new" item.

C4. Send parents to one guide through questions

Two questions (child's phone, then parent's phone if it's an iPhone) lead to exactly one of the three guides. The full list stays below for anyone who wants it. Suggestion: keep this, and test it in the pilot.

C5. Task list on the Start page

The task list gives each family 3 things to do. Should the statuses be real (worked out from what they've done on the site), or fixed text? If real, see D2.

C6. Store the allowed-apps list once

The allowed-apps list appears in every guide, and on the guides page. Store it once so that adding an app (after a request, see the Get help page) updates every guide at once. WhatsApp and school apps are still [TBC] from the phase 1 research.

C7. What if a delay-group child gets a smartphone?

Should the delay group see the restriction guides as a fallback? That's reasonable for families, but it adds a third path to the analysis. For now the mock-up asks them to tell us, and hides the guides. This needs agreeing with IFF and Cambridge.

C8. PDFs

In the mock-up, "Print or save as PDF" uses a print stylesheet, so the PDF always matches the web page. For the paper onboarding pack, export the same page. Suggestion: no separately designed PDFs, because they drift out of date.

C9. Android brands

Brand tips inline in the guide (simpler to keep up to date), or a "Which brand?" question that shows brand-specific steps (easier to follow)? Suggestion: tips inline, covering the 3 most common brands in the pilot sample.

C10. Are basic (non-smart) phones fine in the delay group?

This needs a clear yes or no from the research team, stated plainly in the delay guide. Parents will ask.

V. Video

V1. Vimeo set-up

Vimeo is a good fit: no ads and no recommended videos. On a paid plan (Standard or above), videos can be hidden on vimeo.com, set to play only on our domain, captioned (with checked captions uploaded), and view counts are available per video. The mock-up loads the player only when someone presses Play, with dnt=1, so no Vimeo cookies are set before then.

Suggestion: Vimeo Standard, domain-restricted, with a transcript under every video.

V2. One video per guide, or one per step?

The mock-up uses one video per guide with "play from step X" buttons. Short clips per step are easier to re-record one at a time, but mean more files. Suggestion: one per guide for the pilot. Look again if updates keep changing single steps.

V3. Webinar recordings per group

Restrict and delay families see different recordings. Each has chapters, so parents can skip to the part they need.

V4. Video views per family

Vimeo counts views per video, not per family. To count views per family, the site records a "played" event against the family ID (through Vimeo's player API). See D1.

S. Screen time screenshots

S1. Which screen, and how often?

Ask for one standard screen (the weekly report) so the automatic reading works reliably. The mock-up asks once a month. Frequency and timing need agreeing with IFF's survey schedule.

S2. Step count and sleep score from smartwatches

Suggestion: leave these out of the first version. Keep the form to screen time, with typed-in numbers as an alternative.

S3. Build the pipeline, or use a form tool?

  • Build our own (as in the mock-up): an upload tied to the signed-in family ID, UK storage (for example, AWS London or Azure UK South), image metadata removed on upload, Gemini API processing on PUBLIC's paid terms, a sample checked by hand, and images deleted after the numbers are extracted.
  • Qualtrics (via IFF) or Tally: quicker, and IFF may already have a DPA in place. The family ID is passed in the link, and the form looks less joined-up with the site.

Suggestion: build our own for the main trial, so it can be offered wherever families are (site, newsletter, IFF surveys) with one data store. For the pilot, a Qualtrics form is enough.

S4. Confirmation email

An email after each upload reassures parents, but adds to the messages they get. Suggestion: no email. Show the on-screen confirmation and the "What you've sent before" list instead.

D. Engagement data

D1. Google Analytics, or our own activity log?

The spec asks for Google Analytics and clicks and views "identified to user". Google's terms don't allow data that identifies people to be sent to Google Analytics, and it needs cookie consent, so families who reject cookies would leave gaps in the compliance data.

Suggestion: record the few events that matter for compliance (sign-in, guide opened, step ticked, video played, screenshot uploaded) in our own UK database, against the family ID, as part of the research data described in the privacy notice. If page-level statistics are needed too, use a cookie-free tool (such as Plausible, hosted in the EU) with no family IDs. Check this with the DPIA.

D2. Guide progress as a compliance signal

The "I've done this step" ticks help parents. Recorded against the family ID, "ticked all 9 steps" is a good indicator that restrictions were set up. Is that acceptable to parents, and should the site say so next to the ticks?

N. Newsletter and nudges

N1. What unlocks the next comic chapter?

  • Each newsletter (as in the mock-up): every family gets the next 3 pages on the same day. It's simple and fair, and it gives a reason to open the newsletter, so opens become an engagement signal.
  • Each family's compliance (for example, after a screenshot upload): a stronger nudge, but it rewards or penalises children for what their parents do, and complicates the DPIA and ethics review.

Suggestion: unlock by newsletter. Check with IFF and Cambridge that the comic counts as part of the intervention package.

N2. Tone, jokes and sign-off

"Where's Margot?" is a school adventure (The Goonies meets Indiana Jones over one lunch break), not a story about phones. Meme lines ("+1000 aura", "it's giving") are kept to a few per issue and are easy to swap, because slang dates fast. The pool is drained, nobody gets hurt, and adults are kind but firm ("next time, ask"). Suggestion: DfE signs off the story bible (content/comic-story-bible.md) and the character sheet before chapter 1 is drawn. Test issue 1 with children in the pilot.

N3. One comic for both groups

No phones appear (it all happens in school), so restrict and delay families can share the same story. The cast is diverse in background, year group and ability. Maisie is a wheelchair user, and that isn't the point of her story. Control families never see it, as it sits behind sign-in.

N4. Who makes the comic for real?

The character sheet (draft 3) is drawn in code in a design-first indie style (soft shapes, no outlines, risograph palette), and opens in Figma with editable text. For 48 pages, the options are: carry on in this style, or brief an illustrator with the story bible and the sheet. The same illustrator could make the children's video and the printed pack (see B5).

E. Scope

E1. Sections in the spec that aren't in the mock-up

"Month by month", a children's section, the newsletter archive and the forum. The mock-up includes one children's video and "talking to your child" tips in the guides and FAQs. Suggestion: add the newsletter archive when the first newsletter goes out, and keep the others for after the pilot.

E2. FAQs and the forum

If the Discourse forum goes ahead, the FAQs become the forum's most common questions, with a link to the forum. If not, the FAQs and the support email are all the support families get.
